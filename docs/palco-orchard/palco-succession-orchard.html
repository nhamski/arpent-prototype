01

The site, honestly

The parcel you sent is the USD 269 lot in Palco, Rooks County — the county layer reads 0.49 acres, not the three-quarters you remembered. That difference is real money and real plant count, so everything below is built for 0.49. Walk the corners before we order; every spacing in this plan scales off a nominal 145′ × 145′.

It’s a former playground, and that is the single most important fact about it. "Just dirt" on a playground means compacted dirt — typically packed hard to 8–12 inches by decades of feet, with organic matter near zero and possibly a layer of imported sand or road base under the surface. Trees planted into that will sit in an auger-glazed hole and sulk for five years. Ripping the row lines with a single-shank subsoiler this fall, while the soil is dry, is the highest-value $80 of diesel in the entire project.

Underneath, expect Harney silt loam — Kansas’s state soil, deep and genuinely fertile, but calcareous and moderately alkaline in the B horizon. That single trait does more to shape the plant list than the cold does. It’s why there are no blueberries and no chestnuts in this plan, and why the rootstock column in the tables below is not decoration.

On skipping the soil test

You said we probably don’t need one. You’re likely right about fertility — Harney doesn’t need help there. But the test also returns pH and soluble salts, and on a compacted former playground with possible construction fill, that’s the number that decides whether apples go on MM.111 or something else, and whether we budget elemental sulfur. It’s about $25 a sample through K‑State’s Rooks County office. I’ve carried two samples at $50 in the budget. Pull them in October; if pH comes back under 7.5 we change nothing and you’ve spent fifty dollars on certainty.

02

The plan

Five rows running east–west, perpendicular to the prevailing northwest winter wind, so each row shelters the one behind it. Tallest on the north, shortest on the south, which keeps every row in full sun and throws the longest shadows off our own ground. Rows are 32 feet apart — at maturity that leaves 10 to 14 feet of open, mowable, chicken-runnable alley, which is what "not super dense" actually means in numbers.

Six positions are drawn as open circles. Those are deliberately empty, reserved for trees the students will grow themselves. More on that in section 04.

03

What goes where

Every plant here clears four bars: hardy to 5b, tolerant of alkaline soil, productive on 22 inches of rain plus drip, and unbothered by a caretaker who visits every other week. Beyond that, no two adjacent trees are the same species — that’s Sobkowiak’s rule, and on a small lot it does more for pest and disease pressure than any spray program the school would realistically run.

04

Succession: seed, cutting, graft

This is the part of the brief that reshaped everything else. You’re not planting sacrificial nurse trees to cut down later — you’re planting parents, and the students grow the replacements. That makes "how does this plant reproduce?" a selection criterion equal to hardiness, and it’s why the list above leans so hard on natives and seedling-grown stock.

It also splits the teaching program into three tracks of genuinely different difficulty, which is convenient, because the school spans K through 12.

seed
Track A · Seed

Elementary and middle school. Collect fruit in fall, clean the pits, cold-stratify 90–120 days in damp sand in a refrigerator, sow in the nursery bed in spring.

  • Sandhill plum, chokecherry, Nanking cherry
  • Persimmon, hazelnut, mulberry
  • Peach (~80% true to parent)
  • Aronia (apomictic — a true clone)
  • Buffaloberry, Siberian pea shrub
cutting
Track B · Cuttings

Middle school. Cut pencil-thick dormant wood in February, stick it in sand, water it. Elderberry and grape root at close to 100% with no hormone and no equipment.

  • Elderberry, aronia
  • Currant, gooseberry
  • Grape
  • Mulberry (softwood, under mist)
graft
Track C · Grafting

High school ag. Apples and pears do not come true from seed — every named variety on earth is a clone. Cut dormant scion from the orchard’s own trees in February, whip-and-tongue onto purchased rootstock, grow one season, plant out.

  • All 6 apple varieties
  • Both pear varieties
  • Rootstock: Antonovka or MM.111, ~$3–4 each in bundles of 25

How the gaps actually fill

Six positions are held open from day one — two in row 2, one in row 3, two in row 4, one in row 5. They are not accidents of spacing; they’re marked on the ground with a post and a plaque from the day the orchard goes in. Seed sown in spring 2027 spends two to three years in the nursery bed, which puts the first student-grown tree in the ground in spring 2030. A class that starts seventh grade the year the orchard is planted graduates the year its own trees start bearing.

Row 1 is the exception and the demonstration: sandhill plum and chokecherry sucker and self-seed, so that row repairs itself with no human involvement at all. It’s worth pointing at when explaining to a board why the rest of the orchard needs a nursery bed.

A second product line, free

A nursery bed producing more trees than six slots can absorb produces surplus — and surplus fruit trees sell at a school spring plant sale for $20 to $35 apiece. The same bed that fills the orchard’s gaps can fund the orchard’s maintenance. That’s two revenue streams from one system: the jams, and the trees that made them.

Harvest against the school calendar

This drove a lot of variety selection. A school orchard that ripens in June is a school orchard nobody picks. Wherever there was a choice — fall-bearing raspberry over summer-bearing, late apples over early, persimmon at all — the plan takes the later crop.

The result: the weight of the harvest lands mid-August through October, with students on site. What does ripen over the summer break is, without exception, a crop that freezes well.

The summer problem, solved cheaply

Six crops ripen while school is out, and all six — cherries, currants, gooseberries, serviceberries, blackberries, Nanking cherries — freeze perfectly and lose nothing that matters for jam. Pick them into the school’s freezer over the summer with whatever help is around, then run the jelly batches with students in September. A chest freezer is the cheapest piece of processing equipment in this whole enterprise, and it converts your worst scheduling constraint into a September lab exercise.

Every zone runs on well under 3 gpm, so a standard garden hydrant at 5–10 gpm carries any of them without pressure loss, and two zones can run simultaneously if a timer fails and you need to catch up by hand.

The stack at each hydrant, in order

hydrant → battery timer → hose-bib vacuum breaker → 155-mesh Y-filter → 25 psi pressure regulator → ½″ poly mainline → laterals

Order matters. The filter goes ahead of the regulator, and the vacuum breaker is not optional — it’s a backflow device on a potable school supply, and it’s a $12 part.

Schedule

  • Year 1 (2027): three runs a week, 60–90 minutes, roughly 8–10 gallons per tree per week. Trees are living on the root ball they arrived with; this is the year they die if anything goes wrong.
  • Years 2–3: twice weekly, longer runs, deeper. Add two more emitters per tree as the root zone widens.
  • Year 4 on: deep soak every 10–14 days in July and August only. Mark Shepard would tell you to cut the water off entirely and let the weak ones die; on a school site with $35 trees and a public audience, I’d keep the drip and let it earn its cost back in survival.

What will actually go wrong

Two things, both of them boring. A dead timer battery in July kills more young orchards than drought does — so the biweekly walkthrough starts with pressing the manual-on button and watching for water, not with looking at the trees. And failing to winterize splits the filter housing and the regulator every time. Before November 15: pull timers, filters and regulators indoors, open the ends of every lateral, and let the lines gravity-drain. Put both on the ag teacher’s calendar now, because neither is intuitive and both are expensive.

07

Ground: compaction, mulch, alleys

Rip it this fall

Pull a single-shank subsoiler 18–24 inches deep down each of the five row lines in October or November, when the soil is dry enough to shatter rather than smear. One pass, or two crossing passes at the tree positions. This fractures the playground pan, gives roots somewhere to go, and lets winter moisture in. If a district patron has a tractor with a ripper, this costs fuel and an afternoon; it is worth more than every amendment in the budget combined.

Mulch

Four inches of arborist wood chips in a five-foot strip down each row — call it 50 cubic yards. Do not buy this. Line-clearing crews, county Road & Bridge, the city brush pile, and any tree service within thirty miles will deliver chips free to get rid of them. Start asking in September and stockpile all winter. I’ve carried $275 for trucking and handling, not material.

Lay cardboard under the chips on the first application — the lot will grow a spectacular weed crop the first summer, and cardboard buys a full season. Keep chips four inches clear of every trunk; mulch piled against bark rots the crown and kills the tree in year three, and it is the single most common way a well-funded planting fails.

Alleys

Bare playground dirt will blow and wash all winter if we leave it. Two-stage fix:

  • Fall 2026, right after ripping: broadcast cereal rye and hairy vetch across the whole lot. Cheap, fast, holds the soil, and the vetch fixes nitrogen for the trees arriving in spring.
  • June 2027, once soil is warm: seed the alleys to buffalograss and blue grama with Dutch white clover. Buffalograss is the right answer here and almost nowhere else — it’s the native shortgrass, it survives on 22 inches, it tops out at four to six inches so mowing becomes optional, and the clover feeds both the soil and the chickens.

Trunk protection

Two things, neither expensive, both fatal to skip in Kansas. Rabbit guards — quarter-inch hardware cloth cylinders or spiral wraps — on every trunk, because a rabbit will ring-bark a young apple under snow in one January night. And white latex paint, diluted 1:1, on the southwest side of every trunk. Winter sun on a dark trunk on a cold clear day heats the bark, the cambium wakes, night drops it to zero, and the bark splits. Southwest injury is the leading cause of young-tree death on the High Plains and the fix costs about thirty dollars for the whole orchard.

08

Chickens — when, and how

You don’t have their plan yet, so I’ve designed the orchard to accept birds rather than depend on them. Nothing below is costed in section 10; add roughly $300 for netting and an energizer when the school decides.

  • Keep birds out entirely through 2028. A first- and second-year tree’s roots are in the top eight inches — precisely where chickens scratch — and a fresh mulch ring is an irresistible dust bath. Two seasons of exclusion is the difference between an orchard and an expensive lesson.
  • From 2029, run them in the alleys. A 164-foot roll of electric poultry netting makes a paddock out of one alley. Move every 7 to 14 days. The 32-foot row spacing in this plan exists partly so a netting roll fits an alley without touching branches.
  • Food safety sets the real constraint. Fruit going to a school cafeteria under raw manure wants a harvest interval — the widely used standard is 90 days for crops that don’t touch soil. Simplest workable rule: birds live in the alleys and come off any block 90 days before its fruit is picked. Run this past whoever writes the district’s food-safety plan before the first bird arrives, not after.
  • Two plants are in this design specifically for them. Siberian pea shrub in row 1 drops high-protein seed pods; the mulberry at the west end of row 2 drops fruit for six weeks straight. Both are chicken feed the orchard produces for free.
09

What we’re deliberately not planting

Worth having in writing, because every one of these will get suggested by someone.

Blueberries
Need soil pH 4.5–5.5. Harney’s subsoil is calcareous — you cannot acidify your way out of that in open ground, and people who try spend years watching yellow plants die. Containers of peat, or not at all.
Chestnuts
Same problem. Chinese chestnut goes chlorotic above pH 7 and never recovers. It’s the classic permaculture-orchard staple that simply doesn’t apply to this soil.
Pawpaws
Want 35+ inches of rain, acidic soil, and shade for the first two years. Wrong biome by a wide margin.
Pecans
Genuinely native to Kansas — in the southeast corner, at 35 inches of rain and a longer season. At 22 inches and ~155 frost-free days, Rooks County won’t fill the nuts reliably.
Black walnut
The hard one. It’s the best-adapted nut tree in the county and it would thrive. But it reaches 60–80 feet and its juglone suppresses apples, tomatoes and much else within about 60 feet. On 0.49 acres that’s an either/or, and the orchard is worth more than the walnut. If the district has ground elsewhere, plant it there.
Sweet cherries
Crack in rain, rot in humidity, and lose their crop to spring frost. Tart cherries do everything the kitchen needs and are half the trouble.
June-bearing strawberries
Ripen when school is out, and chickens will find them in an afternoon. If the school wants strawberries, put day-neutrals in a separate raised bed outside the poultry area.
Russian olive and autumn olive
Excellent nitrogen fixers, widely recommended in older permaculture literature, and genuinely invasive in Kansas riparian corridors. Siberian pea shrub and buffaloberry do the same job without the liability.

Where the money is hiding

  • Conservation seedlings. Fifty plants for $105. The Kansas Forest Service Pheasant Bundle is 25 sandhill plum and 25 chokecherry for about $40 — roughly $1.60 a plant against $12 for the same species from a retail nursery. They arrive as 12–18″ whips rather than the 4–5′ stock you wanted, but for a hedge that’s actually better: small conservation stock establishes faster and catches up within three years.
  • Free wood chips. Fifty yards bought retail is $1,500 or more. Asked for in September, it’s the cost of the diesel to haul it.
  • The existing fence as a trellis. Grapes need a wire and a post; they don’t need a structure if there’s already one there.
