Live Cattle Auction Prices — Updated Daily from USDA

August 2026

If you are selling calves this fall or buying replacements, the single most valuable thing you can look at before loading the trailer is what cattle actually brought at auction yesterday. Not last week's average. Not a futures chart. The actual reported price per hundredweight from the sale barns that ran cattle through the ring.

That data exists, it is free, and most ranchers never see it until they are standing at the sale barn reading the board.

What USDA AMS market reports actually are

The USDA Agricultural Marketing Service stations reporters at livestock auctions across the country. After each sale, they compile a market report listing the volume sold, the price range, and the weighted average price for each class of cattle. These reports are published through the Livestock Mandatory Reporting system and are available to anyone.

If you run cattle in Kansas, the report you want is USDA AMS Report 1895 — the Kansas Direct Slaughter Cattle report. It covers negotiated trade, formula trade, and forward contract prices for fed cattle sold direct to packers. Other states and regions have their own report numbers, but the structure is the same: class, weight range, price, and volume.

Each report breaks cattle into categories that matter at the pen level. You will see separate lines for steers and heifers, broken out by weight class — 400-500 lb calves, 500-600 lb calves, 600-700 lb feeders, and so on up through finished weights. The report shows the price range (low to high) and the weighted average for each group. That average is what most buyers and sellers reference when they talk about "today's cattle prices."

Why daily prices matter for timing

Cattle prices move. Not like grain futures that tick every second, but meaningfully from week to week. A $3/cwt swing on a 550-pound calf is $16.50 a head. Run that across 80 calves and you are looking at $1,320 that appeared or disappeared because you sold on Tuesday instead of the following Monday.

Watching the daily USDA cattle market data gives you a read on which direction the market is drifting. If feeder steer prices have dropped $4/cwt over the past two weeks, that is useful information before you commit to a sale date. If heifer prices are holding steady while steer prices soften, that might change how you sort your calves before shipping.

None of this is about trying to time the absolute top. It is about not walking into the ring blind.

Seasonal patterns worth watching

Cattle prices follow patterns that repeat most years, even if the exact levels shift. Feeder calf prices typically peak in spring when grass buyers are competing for inventory and dip in the fall when the big calf runs hit. Finished cattle tend to firm up ahead of grilling season and soften into late autumn as packer schedules fill.

The rancher who watches daily auction data through the year starts to see these patterns in real time — not just on a chart someone published in a magazine three months later. You can see when your local market starts to bid up 5-weight steers in March, and you can see when it levels off. That is the kind of signal that helps you decide whether to background calves another 60 days or sell them now.
