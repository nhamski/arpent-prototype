When to Sell Cattle: A Calculator That Uses Real Market Data

August 2026

Every cattleman knows the feeling. Calves are gaining well, grass is holding, and you start doing the math in your head: sell now while the price is decent, or put on another hundred pounds and hope the market holds? That decision -- when to pull the trigger -- is the single biggest lever on your annual revenue. And most of us make it on gut feel.

There is a better way. But first, you need to understand how the seasonal market actually works, because the calendar is either your friend or your enemy.

The Seasonal Price Cycle

Cattle prices follow a pattern that has held for decades. Spring prices tend to be the highest of the year, typically peaking between March and May. Demand is strong -- feedlots are buying to fill pens, and the supply of calves from the previous fall run has thinned out. Fewer animals available, more buyers competing. Prices go up.

Then fall comes. From September through November, the country weans its calf crop all at once. Auction barns flood with freshly weaned 400- to 550-pound calves. Supply spikes, and prices drop -- sometimes 15 to 20 percent below spring levels on the same weight class. It happens every year. The calves are not worth less; there are just too many of them hitting the market at the same time.

The takeaway is simple: if you can avoid selling into the fall glut, you usually get paid more per pound. But that is only half the equation.

Weight Class Changes Everything

Price per pound and total dollars are two different things, and they move in opposite directions as cattle get heavier. A 450-pound steer calf might bring $2.85 per pound at auction. That same animal at 750 pounds might only bring $2.20. The per-pound price slides as weight goes up -- that is normal, and it reflects the diminishing gain potential the next owner is buying.

But do the math: 450 at $2.85 is $1,282. 750 at $2.20 is $1,650. You made $368 more by adding 300 pounds, even though the price per pound dropped. The question is whether those extra 300 pounds cost you more or less than $368 to put on.

That is the real decision. Not just "what is the market doing" but "what does it cost me to add another pound, and what will the market pay me for it when I get there?"

The Tradeoff: Gain Days vs. Declining Prices

Here is where cattle sell timing gets tricky. Holding cattle longer means more pounds to sell, but it also means you are walking into a different price environment. If you wean in October and hold through winter to sell in March, you pick up both the weight gain and the seasonal price recovery. That can be a strong play -- if your cost of gain cooperates.

Cost of gain depends on your operation. If you are running on grass with low input costs, putting on a pound might cost you $0.60 to $0.80. If you are feeding hay at $200 a ton and supplement on top, you could be looking at $1.20 or more per pound of gain. When your cost of gain exceeds what the market will pay for that extra weight, you are losing money by holding.

The best time to sell cattle is when the spread between your cost of gain and the market value of that gain starts to close. That point is different for every operation, every year, and every weight class. Which is exactly why a calculator helps.

Why This Matters More Than It Used To

Feed costs are volatile. Hay markets are regional and unpredictable. Cattle prices have been at historic highs but the spreads between weight classes have shifted. The old rules of thumb -- "sell in April" or "never sell in October" -- were never precise, and they are even less reliable now. The ranchers making the best decisions are the ones running the numbers with current data, not last year's.

That does not mean you need a spreadsheet or a commodities degree. It means you need a tool that does the math for you, using prices that are current and costs that are yours.
